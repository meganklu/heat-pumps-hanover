How they work & why they help

The benefits of heat pumps

Heat pumps extract existing thermal energy from the outside air or ground and transfer it indoors or outdoors, depending on whether a building needs heating or cooling. Because they move heat instead of generating it by burning fuel, they can deliver more energy to a home than the electricity they consume.

See it in motion

How a heat pump moves heat

In winter mode, the outdoor unit pulls existing thermal energy out of the cold outside air. The refrigerant loop carries that heat inside, where it's released to warm the home.

In summer mode, the indoor unit pulls heat out of the warm air inside the home. The refrigerant loop carries that heat outside, where it's released, cooling the home in the process.

Two ways to do it

Air-source vs. ground-source

Every heat pump shown so far is an air-source system — it exchanges heat with the outside air, the most common and affordable setup for New England homes. A ground-source (geothermal) system instead exchanges heat with a buried loop, tapping into the earth's stable year-round temperature for even higher efficiency — at a much higher installation cost and a need for yard space most rental properties in Hanover simply don't have.

Air-source

  • Heat exchange with Outside air
  • Efficiency* 3–4× more efficient
  • Install cost Lower
  • Space needed None extra

Ground-source (geothermal)

  • Heat exchange with Buried loop in the earth
  • Efficiency* 4–5× more efficient
  • Install cost Higher
  • Space needed Yard or borehole access

*Compared to electric resistance (baseboard) heating, which converts electricity to heat at a 1:1 ratio.

Why it matters

What renters and landlords actually notice

Lower energy bills

Because heat pumps move heat rather than generate it, they typically deliver 3–4 times more heating energy than the electricity they use. This is far more efficient than electric baseboard or resistance heating, and it's often cheaper to run than oil or propane.

Heating and cooling, one system

A single heat pump replaces both a furnace and a separate air conditioner, running heat in the winter and reversing to cool in the summer. That's one system to install, maintain, and budget for instead of two.

Lower emissions

Heat pumps run entirely on electricity, with no on-site combustion of oil, propane, or natural gas. As New England's electric grid gets cleaner, the emissions from heating a home with a heat pump keep falling too.

Simpler, safer operation

No combustion means no fuel deliveries, no flue or chimney to maintain, and no carbon monoxide risk from the heating system itself—a meaningful safety upgrade for older rental housing stock.

Better comfort and air quality

Modern heat pumps deliver more even, consistent temperatures than baseboard heat or window units, with better humidity control and less of the temperature swing renters describe with older systems.

Rising value over time

As utility rates for oil and propane continue to be volatile, an efficient electric system insulates a property (and its energy bills) from those swings. Thus, heat pumps are a long-term asset for the building owner.

How a furnace works

Furnaces burn fuel—oil, propane, or gas—to generate heat directly, then blow that warm air into the home. Some of the energy released by combustion always escapes as exhaust up the flue, and the whole system only runs in one direction: there's no way to reverse a furnace to cool a home in the summer.

  • Burns fuel on-site
  • Heating only, no cooling
  • Wastes heat as exhaust
For comparison

How an air conditioning unit works

A window unit is electric like a heat pump, but it only moves heat in one direction. It pulls warmth out of a room and rejects it outside to cool the space. Central AC works the same way at whole-home scale, using ductwork instead of a single room unit. Either way, there's no reverse mode, so AC is typically paired with a separate furnace for winter heat rather than replacing it the way a heat pump can.

  • Electric, no combustion
  • Cooling only, no heat
  • Usually paired with a furnace

Incentives already available in New Hampshire

NHSaves—a collaboration between Eversource, Liberty, New Hampshire Electric Co-op, and Unitil—already offers information, incentives, and support for households installing efficient electric heating and cooling equipment statewide. The NEHPA Community Grant is designed to build on programs like this specifically for renters and moderate-income households.
