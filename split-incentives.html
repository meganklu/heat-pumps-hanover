The core barrier

Why the best upgrade doesn't always happen

Heat pumps pay for themselves over time through lower energy bills — but in a rental property, the person who pays the upfront cost usually isn't the person who receives those savings. Economists call this the "split incentive" problem, and it's one of the biggest reasons rental housing lags behind owner-occupied homes in adopting efficient technology.

The mismatch

Two different people, two different incentives

🏠

The landlord

Pays the upfront cost of installing a heat pump, but typically doesn't pay the utility bill — so the ongoing energy savings flow to the tenant, not the owner. Without a way to recover that investment, many landlords default to the cheapest available system when something breaks, rather than the most efficient one.

👤

The renter

Pays the energy bill and would benefit most directly from an efficient system, but has no authority to make capital improvements to a property they don't own — and little reason to invest in equipment they may not use for more than a year or two.

In Hanover, specifically

Why this is especially relevant here

A large, price-sensitive rental market

The average rental price across Hanover is roughly $3,200 — about 40–60% above the national average — with heavy demand driven in part by student housing. That combination makes both landlords and tenants sensitive to any cost that isn't clearly justified.

Short tenancies, long-lived equipment

Hanover restricts most short-term rentals, so units mostly serve long-term residents — but individual tenants, especially students, often turn over far faster than the 15–20 year lifespan of an HVAC system, weakening any single tenant's incentive to advocate for an upgrade.

An existing oversight framework

Every rental unit in Hanover must be registered with the Town and periodically inspected to maintain a Certificate of Compliance (COC). That administrative system already identifies who owns and manages every rental property — a ready-made channel for targeting heat pump outreach and incentives.

What tends to help

Ways to close the gap

Communities that have made progress on this problem generally attack it from a few directions at once:

  • 1

    Lower the landlord's upfront cost

    Grants, rebates, and low- or no-interest financing (like the NEHPA Community Grant) reduce or eliminate the capital outlay, so the split-incentive math no longer has to work out purely on utility savings.

  • 2

    Share the benefit explicitly

    Some programs pair funding with lease terms or agreements that let landlords recover part of their investment (for example, through a modest, capped rent adjustment) while still leaving tenants better off overall.

  • 3

    Use existing compliance touchpoints

    Tying outreach and incentive offers to a process landlords already go through — like Hanover's rental registration and inspection cycle — lowers the effort needed to reach eligible properties and makes participation feel like a natural extension of existing obligations, not an extra task.

  • 4

    Bring everyone to the table

    Programs that involve landlords, tenants, contractors, and local government together — rather than designing a fix for renters in isolation — tend to surface concerns early and build the buy-in needed for a program to actually get used.

This is exactly what the NEHPA grant is meant to address

The stated purpose of our section of the grant proposal is "reducing obstacles for renters to access the benefits of heat pump technology." We're using survey and interview data from Hanover renters, landlords, and property managers to shape a proposal that reflects what actually gets in the way here — not just the barrier in the abstract.
